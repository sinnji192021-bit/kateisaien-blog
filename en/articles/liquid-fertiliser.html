Liquid fertiliser 💧 Weak, and little and often

November 11, 2026SoilAbout 15 min

💧 "My spinach sown in autumn is yellowish and just sits there."

💧 "My aubergines are flagging at the end of summer. Will a feed bring them back?"

💧 "I made it stronger so it would work faster, and the plants wilted."

Hello, I'm Eiko. Eight years of growing vegetables organically and without pesticides, and a teaching licence in science.

Liquid fertiliser is the quick fix of the vegetable garden. Because it's already dissolved, it reaches the roots almost as soon as it soaks in, and leaves can visibly green up within days. But "fast" cuts both ways: it also runs out fast, and too strong a mix damages the very roots you're trying to feed.

This article covers how liquid feed differs from solid fertiliser, when it's the right tool and when it isn't, how to tell real hunger from thirst or root damage, how to dilute it and where to pour it, making your own, and how to use it crop by crop.

Where you are: in Japan, liquid feeds are sold as concentrates diluted 500 or 1,000 times, and homemade feeds are usually brewed from aburakasu (the cake left after pressing rapeseed or soybeans for oil) or from composted poultry manure pellets. Elsewhere, look for a general-purpose or tomato liquid feed, or fish, seaweed or comfrey-based organic feeds. For homemade versions, any seed meal (rapeseed, soybean, cottonseed) behaves much like aburakasu, and pelleted chicken manure works like the poultry manure here. Always follow the dilution printed on your product: strengths vary by brand.

The essentials: use solid fertiliser as the base and liquid feed as first aid; dilute exactly as the label says (1:500 is about 2 ml per litre, or 1½ teaspoons per US gallon), and weaker if unsure; pour it where the roots end — under the outer edge of the leaves — not at the stem; feed again only after a week; skip it on cold, wet winter soil, before heavy rain and in the heat of the day.

① Liquid vs solid fertiliser at a glance

Liquid fertiliser

  • Form: already in solution, or a concentrate you dilute with water
  • Speed: fast — leaf colour can change within a few days
  • How long it lasts: short. Often gone in one to two weeks
  • How you apply it: with a watering can, like watering
  • Best for: side dressing, emergencies, containers, a last push before the cold

Solid fertiliser (granular, seed meal, poultry manure)

  • Form: granules or powder
  • Speed: chemical granules are fairly quick; seed meal and manure must be broken down in the soil first, so they're slow
  • How long it lasts: weeks to over a month
  • How you apply it: scatter round the plant and scratch it lightly into the soil
  • Best for: base dressing before planting and regular side dressing

How to think about the two

Routine feeding: solid. Emergencies, or when it's too cold for solid fertiliser to work: liquid. Growing on liquid feed alone means feeding again and again. In a home garden it's far easier to build on solid fertiliser and keep liquid feed as first aid for when something's short.

② Kinds of liquid fertiliser

Shop-bought

  • Concentrates are diluted with water. A small bottle goes a long way, so these suit a vegetable plot
  • Ready-to-use feeds are pre-diluted. Handy for a few containers, but expensive for the volume
  • Soluble powders and crystals are dissolved in water before use, and keep well

Reading the label

Three numbers such as "6-10-5" give the percentages of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K), in that order.

  • Nitrogen grows leaves and stems — for leafy crops and pale leaves
  • Phosphorus drives flowering and fruiting — for tomatoes, aubergines, cucumbers and other fruiting crops
  • Potassium builds roots and toughens the plant — for root crops and tired late-summer plants

In the 6-10-5 example, phosphorus is highest, so it's a fruiting-crop feed. A feed with the first number highest is for leafy crops; three similar numbers make a general-purpose feed.

"Plant tonics" are not fertiliser

On the same shelf you'll often find root stimulants and tonics. Most contain little or none of the three main nutrients and can't stand in for fertiliser. When a plant is short of food, choose a product that states its N-P-K.

Homemade

Seed meal or poultry manure steeped and fermented in water. Cheap, and suits organic growers.

  • Seed meal feed: nitrogen-rich — for pale leaves and leafy crops
  • Poultry manure feed: higher in phosphorus — for fruiting crops and the later stages of growth

How to make them, and the safety points, come further down.

③ Is it really hunger? Check first

Before feeding, make sure the plant really is short of fertiliser. Thirst and root damage look surprisingly similar, and a feed won't fix either.

Signs of nitrogen shortage

  • Older, lower leaves turn evenly yellow first
  • New leaves come out pale and yellow-green
  • Leaves are small and the whole plant grows slowly
  • It's a size smaller than the same crop nearby

Signs of thirst

  • Leaves wilt in the middle of the day and recover by evening or morning
  • Dig a finger 5 cm (2 in) into the soil: it's dry and crumbly all the way down
  • Leaf colour isn't particularly bad

Here the answer is a thorough watering, not a feed.

Signs of too much fertiliser

  • Leaves are dark green and the plant is all foliage
  • Flowers appear but fruit doesn't set
  • Aphids and other sap-suckers are multiplying

A feed would make this worse. Hold off fertiliser for a while.

Signs of root damage

  • Wilting doesn't recover after watering
  • The plant wobbles at the base, or the bottom of the stem is discoloured
  • Grubs (such as chafer larvae) turn up in the soil

Damaged roots can't take up food, so feeding won't help. Deal with the pest or disease first.

If you're not sure

Check watering first, then look for pests and disease. If the lower leaves are still yellowing after that, treat it as hunger and try one liquid feed. Wait about a week: the colour of the new growth will tell you whether that was the problem.

④ When liquid feed is the right tool

1. Pale leaves that need help quickly

Yellowing from the bottom up and yellow-green new leaves are classic nitrogen shortage. Solid fertiliser takes time to kick in; liquid feed makes up the shortfall fast.

2. Autumn, as it gets colder

As temperatures fall, soil microbes slow down and organic fertilisers like seed meal and manure break down less readily. Side dress with solid fertiliser in the second half of October (in central Japan) and it may only start working once the cold has arrived. If autumn-sown greens or radishes are lagging, a liquid feed before the real cold gives them one more spurt.

3. Containers

Containers hold little soil, and every watering washes nutrients out of the drainage holes, so they run short easily. Liquid feed lets you side dress as you water.

4. Plants flagging at the end of summer

Aubergines, peppers and other long-season crops can be worn out by heat and drought. If the roots are sound and new shoots and flowers are still appearing, a liquid feed can revive them for an autumn harvest. A plant with no new growth or flowers at all is unlikely to recover, feed or no feed.

5. When there's nowhere to put granules

Under plastic mulch, or among crowded plants, you can lift the edge of the mulch and pour liquid feed underneath.

6. Seedlings that are starving

Seedlings kept in pots for a while can exhaust the little compost they have and yellow at the base. Feed them at even weaker than the label strength: small roots are easily burnt by strong solutions.

⑤ When not to use it

Cold, wet winter soil

Winter soil dries slowly. Adding more liquid keeps the roots waterlogged. For overwintering crops such as onions and garlic, feed when the soil is dry, or use a little solid fertiliser instead.

The heat of a summer day

Roots in hot soil may already be stressed. If you feed in summer, do it in the cool of the morning or evening — and remember that in high summer, not letting plants dry out matters more.

Just before heavy rain

Because it's dissolved, heavy rain washes liquid feed away before the roots can take it up. Check the forecast and avoid the day before rain.

As a base dressing

It doesn't last, so it can't replace the compost and fertiliser you work in before planting.

Crops that suffer from too much nitrogen

Chinese cabbage and cabbage given too much nitrogen late on can make leaves without forming a head. Carrots and radishes with too much leaf don't fatten their roots. If the leaves are dark and healthy, they don't need a feed.

⑥ Using shop-bought liquid feed

1. Dilute exactly as the label says

The most important rule. Making it stronger "so it works better" damages the roots. If in doubt, go weaker.

  • 1:500 — 2 ml per litre of water (about 1½ teaspoons per US gallon)
  • 1:1,000 — 1 ml per litre (about ¾ teaspoon per US gallon)

Measure with a measuring cup, a dropper or the marks on the bottle cap. Don't just glug it in.

2. Feed when the soil is on the dry side

Roots take it up better if you give it in place of a watering as the surface dries, rather than on soil that's already wet.

3. Aim for the root tips

Roots absorb mainly near their tips, and they usually spread about as wide as the leaves. Don't just pour at the stem: let it soak in slowly round the outer edge of the foliage, a little way out from the plant.

4. Little and often

What works fast runs out fast. Rather than one big dose, give weak doses spaced out. After each, wait about a week and check whether the colour is coming back.

5. Know when to stop

Once the leaves are a good green and the plant is growing, stop. Too much leads to all leaf and no fruit, and attracts pests.

⑦ Feeding with a watering can, step by step

1. Mix in a bucket

Rather than diluting in the can, measure the concentrate into a bucket of water, stir well with a stick, then fill the can. It mixes more evenly.

2. Use the rose and pour slowly

Keep the rose on and pour gently round the plant. A hard stream scours the soil, exposes roots and runs off instead of soaking in.

3. Under plastic mulch

Lift the edge of the mulch and pour a little way out from the plant. Pouring only into the planting hole doesn't reach the root tips.

4. The same amount for each plant

Work along the row giving each plant about the same. It's tempting to give a weak plant more, but adjust by how often, not how much.

5. Rinse the can

A can with feed left in it can water seedlings or a seed row at an unexpected strength. Rinse it well when you're done.

⑧ Making your own

What you need

  • A plastic bottle (500 ml to 2 litres)
  • A funnel (or a rolled-up sheet of paper)
  • A measuring cup and spoon
  • Composted poultry manure, or seed meal
  • A marker pen to write the contents and date on the bottle

One 500 ml bottle lasts a home garden quite a while. Start small and scale up once you know how much you use.

Poultry manure feed

  1. Take a bottle of about 500 ml
  2. Add composted poultry manure to about a tenth of its volume (50 ml or so), using the funnel
  3. Fill with water and shake well
  4. Leave the cap slightly loose and stand it out of the sun
  5. About every three days, tighten the cap, shake well, then loosen it again
  6. After one to two weeks the nutrients have dissolved out. Use the clear liquid at the top

Made from composted manure, it smells relatively little.

Seed meal feed

  1. Put seed meal and water in a bottle at roughly 1 to 10
  2. Shake well and leave the cap slightly loose
  3. Shake now and then
  4. As it ferments the liquid turns dark. Use the clear liquid at the top

Seed meal gives off a strong, ammonia-like smell as it ferments. Adding lactic acid bacteria is said to reduce it, and some people add a splash of a probiotic yoghurt drink for that reason.

The big danger: gas and smell

  • Fermentation makes gas. With the cap screwed tight, the bottle can swell and burst. Keep it loose
  • The smell is strong: don't make it indoors or under a neighbour's window
  • It clings to clothes. Don't put your face close when you open it
  • Keep it out of reach of children and pets, and label it clearly so nobody drinks it

Diluting homemade feed

The brew is diluted again before use. Its strength depends on how much you used and how far it fermented, so there's no fixed ratio as there is for a bought product. Commonly given figures are about 5–10 times for poultry manure feed and a much weaker 100–500 times for seed meal feed. Start weak and adjust by watching the leaves.

When it goes wrong

  • Smells too strong: seed meal feeds do. Try the lactic-bacteria method, or switch to the milder poultry manure feed
  • Bottle swelling: the cap is too tight. Loosen it to let gas out
  • Sediment caking at the bottom: shake now and then, and use only the clear top layer

Storing

Keep it somewhere cool and out of the sun. Don't make more than you can use — one 500 ml bottle is plenty for a home garden.

Which homemade feed for what

  • Pale leaves, or leafy crops you want to push on → nitrogen-rich seed meal feed
  • Fruiting crops, later growth, heading crops → phosphorus-rich poultry manure feed
  • Not sure → poultry manure feed, on the weak side. It's less likely to overdo the nitrogen

Some people make both and mix them half and half before diluting, to give nitrogen and phosphorus together — good for tired aubergines at the end of summer.

⑨ Crop by crop

Aubergines and peppers

When plants tire at the end of summer, combine a solid side dressing with a liquid feed. If the roots are still sound and new shoots and flowers are coming, it can carry them into an autumn harvest. Soak it in well a little way out, round the root tips, and repeat after about a week.

Cucumbers

Plants cropped hard tire and start producing curved or tapering fruit. A weak liquid feed on top of solid side dressing helps them recover. Cucumber roots are shallow and wide, so soak the whole surface of the bed a little way from the plant.

Tomatoes

Use it when signs of hunger appear: pale leaves, yellowing from the bottom. Since getting the dilution wrong can damage roots, beginners may find it safer to bury solid poultry manure about 20 cm (8 in) from the plant. If you do use liquid feed, lift the mulch and pour a little way out.

Carrots

Leaf tips yellowing midway through growth mean nitrogen is short. A lump of solid fertiliser touching a root can make it fork, so liquid feed suits carrots well. Skip it if the tops are already lush.

Spinach, komatsuna and other leafy greens

Give a weak, nitrogen-rich feed when leaves are pale. Too much makes leaves soft and attracts pests, so stop once the colour returns.

Radishes, Chinese cabbage and cabbage

If they're behind in autumn, feed before the cold. Once cabbages start to heart up, avoid too much nitrogen — use something like poultry manure feed instead.

Autumn greens that have fallen behind

Late in autumn, yellow lower leaves and yellow-green new leaves on greens or radishes are textbook nitrogen shortage. Feed before the temperature bottoms out and they can gain another size before harvest. Miss the moment and they stay small.

Broccoli

If you're picking side shoots over a long season, keep side dressing about once a month. In cold weather solid fertiliser breaks down slowly, so liquid feed works faster. Switch back to solid when it warms up.

Onions and garlic

Winter soil is usually wet, so solid fertiliser suits them better. If you use liquid feed, pick a day when the soil is dry.

Containers

Feed at label strength as you water, as often as the label says. Giving enough that it runs out of the drainage holes also flushes out old fertiliser salts.

⑩ Fitting it in with solid fertiliser

Autumn and winter crops (Chinese cabbage, radishes, broccoli)

  • Before planting: compost and base fertiliser
  • From 2–3 weeks after planting: side dress with solid fertiliser
  • Late October to November (central Japan): if they're behind, liquid feed before the cold
  • In the cold: solid fertiliser barely works, so liquid feed if needed

Summer crops (aubergines, peppers, tomatoes)

  • Before planting: compost and base fertiliser
  • Once fruit starts to set: solid side dressing every 2–3 weeks
  • Pale leaves or poor fruit set: liquid feed as first aid
  • End of summer: solid side dressing plus liquid feed for tired plants

Containers

  • Before planting: potting compost with fertiliser added, or mix in base fertiliser
  • Once rooted: liquid feed as you water, as often as the label says
  • Adding a controlled-release fertiliser at label rates cuts the number of liquid feeds

⑪ Through the year (central Japan)

  • April–May: don't feed just-planted seedlings. Once they've rooted and put out new leaves, feed weakly if needed
  • June–July: regularly, as you water, for summer crops in containers
  • August–September: for heat-weary aubergines and peppers, and autumn greens or radishes that are behind
  • October–November: the last push before the cold, when solid fertiliser stops working
  • December–February: hold off when the soil is wet. Only what really needs it, such as broccoli being picked for side shoots

Further north, or wherever the cold arrives earlier, move the autumn push forward to match: aim to feed a few weeks before your usual first frost.

⑫ Where opinions differ

Spraying the leaves (foliar feeding)

Some say very dilute feed can be sprayed on leaves for an even faster effect; others point out it can scorch leaves depending on strength. Homemade feeds also smell, which you may notice when you eat the crop. Feed the soil as a rule, and spray leaves only with a product whose label says it's suitable for foliar use, at the stated rate.

How much to dilute homemade feed

Recommended ratios for the same material vary widely between sources, because the strength of a homemade brew isn't consistent. There's no single right answer. Start weak and adjust as you watch the plants.

Organic or chemical liquid feed?

Organic feeds also feed soil microbes, but they smell and vary in strength. Chemical feeds have a clear analysis, no smell and are easy to handle, but do nothing for the soil. Use chemical for a quick fix, homemade or bought organic feeds if you grow organically — choose by purpose.

If I use a plant tonic, do I need fertiliser?

Tonics are meant to help rooting and the like, but most contain very little nutrient and can't replace fertiliser. When plants are hungry, use a feed with a stated N-P-K, or solid fertiliser.

⑬ Common mistakes

Making it stronger to work faster

It can damage roots and make plants wilt. Weak, and little and often.

Pouring it all at the stem

The thick roots at the base absorb little. Aim for the root tips at the edge of the leaves.

Relying on liquid feed alone

It doesn't last, so you'd be feeding constantly. Build on base fertiliser and solid side dressing, and use liquid as first aid.

Feeding just-planted seedlings

Roots that haven't settled take up little and are easily damaged. Just water at first.

Mistaking a midday wilt for hunger

Wilting at midday that recovers by evening is almost always thirst. If watering doesn't revive it, suspect the roots. Feeding comes after both.

Screwing the bottle cap tight

Fermentation gas can swell and burst the bottle. Keep it loose.

Drenching cold, wet soil in winter

The soil never dries and the roots weaken. In winter, feed when the soil is dry, or use solid fertiliser.

⑭ Questions people ask

How quickly does it work?

In good conditions, leaves darken within a few days to a week. If nothing changes after a week, suspect something other than hunger: thirst, root damage, disease or pests.

Can I feed every day?

That's too much. Follow the label's frequency for bought feeds, and leave about a week between homemade feeds while you watch the result.

The leaves went pale after a run of rain

Prolonged rain can wash nutrients out of the soil. Once it's fine and the soil has dried a little, top up with a weak liquid feed. Avoid it while the soil is still sodden.

Can I keep leftover feed?

A bought concentrate, capped and kept out of the sun, lasts as long as the label says. Once diluted, use it the same day.

Is rice-washing water a liquid feed?

It contains a little of the rice bran's nutrients, but very little as fertiliser goes, and poured on in quantity it can smell or go mouldy. A proper feed or solid fertiliser is more reliable.

How much per plant?

Enough to moisten the soil out to the root tips. Just wetting the surface won't reach the roots, while flooding it beyond the bed is waste. Pour a little at a time and let it soak in.

Do I need to water as well?

No — the feed is the watering. If the soil is bone dry, give a little plain water first so the feed soaks in instead of running off.

I fed, and now there seem to be more insects

More nitrogen makes soft, lush leaves that attract aphids and other sap-suckers. Once the colour is back, stop feeding.

There's a white film on my homemade feed

A white skin can form during fermentation. If it doesn't smell strongly rotten, the clear liquid beneath can usually still be diluted and used. If you're unsure, bury it in the soil rather than using it.

⑮ A checklist before you feed

  • Are the lower leaves yellowing? Have you ruled out thirst and root damage?
  • Are the leaves already too dark, the plant all foliage? (Then don't feed.)
  • Is it a just-planted seedling?
  • Is the soil on the dry side — not cold and wet in winter?
  • Is it the heat of the day? Is heavy rain forecast tomorrow?
  • Did you measure the dilution as the label says — or weaker if unsure?
  • Are you pouring at the edge of the leaves, not at the stem?
  • Has it been about a week since the last feed?

If any of these catches, skip it today, or deal with the real cause first.

🔬 The science: how liquid feed works

Why it works fast Roots can only take up nutrients dissolved in water. Organic fertilisers must first be broken down by soil microbes into soluble forms; even chemical granules have to dissolve in soil water first. Liquid feed is already dissolved, so it's ready for the roots the moment it soaks in.

From seed meal to plant food The nitrogen in seed meal or manure can't be absorbed as it is. Soil microbes first break down proteins into ammonia, then other microbes turn the ammonia into nitrate, the form roots take up most readily. A homemade brew simply runs part of that breakdown in the bottle in advance — which is why it works faster than the same material scattered dry.

Why too strong burns the roots Roots draw water in when the solution inside them is more concentrated than the soil water around them. Pour on a strong feed and the soil water becomes the more concentrated side. Water moves towards the stronger solution, so instead of entering the root it's drawn out of it. This is osmosis, and it's why an over-strong feed makes plants wilt or even die.

Why it helps in the cold The microbes that break down organic fertiliser slow right down as soil temperature falls, so solid organic fertiliser scattered in the cold barely works. Liquid feed doesn't need breaking down, so it reaches the roots regardless.

Why nitrogen shortage starts at the bottom Nitrogen is a building block of chlorophyll, which makes leaves green. When nitrogen runs short, the plant pulls it out of its old lower leaves and sends it to the new growth. So the bottom leaves yellow first — the clearest signal that the plant is running out of food.

Why aim for the root tips Roots absorb water and nutrients mainly through their thin, soft tips, which are covered in fine root hairs that multiply the area in contact with the soil. The thick roots near the stem mostly act as pipes. The tips have usually reached about as far as the outer leaves — so that's where to pour.

What phosphorus and potassium do Phosphorus is involved in moving energy around inside the plant and is used heavily in flowering, fruiting and root growth. Potassium regulates the water in cells and helps move sugars into fruit and roots. That's why feeds high in phosphorus and potassium suit tired late-summer plants and crops that keep on fruiting.

To sum up

  • ✅ Liquid feed works fast and runs out fast. Solid fertiliser as a rule; liquid for emergencies and cold weather
  • ✅ Good for: pale leaves, autumn before the cold, containers, tired late-summer plants
  • ✅ Not for: cold wet winter soil, base dressing, too much nitrogen before cabbages heart up
  • ✅ Dilute as the label says, weaker if unsure. Too strong and water is drawn out of the roots
  • ✅ Pour at the edge of the leaves, towards the root tips — not at the stem
  • ✅ Weak, and little and often. Check after a week
  • ✅ Homemade: mind the gas and smell. Keep the cap loose and the bottle out of the sun
  • ✅ Before feeding, rule out thirst and root damage. A midday wilt that recovers by evening is thirst
  • ✅ Avoid the heat of the day and the day before heavy rain; feed in the morning or evening
