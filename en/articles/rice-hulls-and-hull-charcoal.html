Rice hulls and hull charcoal 🌾 Ten ways to use them

November 9, 2026SoilAbout 15 min

🌾 "I mixed lots of rice hulls into the soil and my vegetables grew worse."

🌾 "I spread them round my plants and the wind blew them all away."

🌾 "What's the difference between the hulls and the black charcoal?"

Hello, I'm Eiko. Eight years of growing vegetables organically and without pesticides, and a teaching licence in science.

In late autumn in rural Japan you'll see mountains of rice hulls piled outside rice mills, sometimes with a "help yourself" sign. Those hulls are a remarkably useful material for the home garden.

But I also hear "I mixed in loads and my vegetables did worse" and "the wind took the lot". This article covers the difference between rice hulls and rice-hull charcoal, how much to mix into soil, how to spread them, using them at sowing, using the charcoal, and what to watch out for.

Where you are: outside rice-growing areas, rice hulls (also sold as "rice husks" or "parboiled rice hulls") are available from garden and hydroponics suppliers, and rice-hull charcoal is sold as "carbonised rice hulls" (CRH), close kin to biochar. Where hulls are hard to get, the same jobs can be done by other materials: straw or chopped bracken for mulching against frost heave, and grit or coarse bark for drainage. Quantities here are given by volume as well as weight, since hulls are so light.

The essentials: mix in no more than about ½ lb per 10 sq ft (250 g per m², or 2–3 litres of hulls) and add something nitrogen-rich with them; mix in at least two weeks before planting; on sandy soil, mulch rather than mix; damp the hulls after spreading so they don't blow; let homemade charcoal cool completely before bagging it.

① At a glance

Rice hulls

  • Looks: pale brown, hard, boat-shaped husks
  • Character: hard, water-repellent, very slow to rot in soil; rich in silica
  • Nutrients: almost no nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium
  • Main uses: mixed into soil for drainage, as mulch, over seed rows, as compost and bokashi material, for storing tubers
  • Where to get them: rice mills, rice farmers, garden centres

Rice-hull charcoal

  • Looks: black, light and free-flowing; the hull shape remains
  • Character: full of tiny pores that house microbes; said to be mildly alkaline
  • Nutrients: a soil improver rather than a fertiliser; a little potassium and other minerals
  • Main uses: mixed into soil and seedling compost, spread as a black mulch, in bokashi
  • Where to get it: garden centres; you can also make it

Which to use

  • Improve a poorly drained plot gradually: rice hulls
  • Mulch against frost, drying or splashing: hulls (when you need a lot); charcoal (when you want to use a little, or warm the soil)
  • Mix into seedling or container compost: charcoal
  • Stop a seed row drying out: either

② About rice hulls

What they are

Rice hulls are the husks around each grain of rice, left over when the grain is hulled to brown rice. They're mostly fibrous carbohydrate, notable for a lot of silica, and contain almost none of the nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium that feed vegetables.

Hard and slow to rot

Pick up a handful and they feel hard and slippery. Silica is a relative of glass, and thanks to it hulls barely break down in soil. They're often still recognisable after a year, and turn up again after two or three. That's a strength and a caution: they keep opening up the soil for a long time, but once you've put in too many, you can't take them out.

Getting hold of them

  • Autumn into winter, after the rice harvest, is when they're easiest to find
  • Rice mills and rice farmers sometimes give them away or sell them cheaply
  • Garden centres sell them bagged
  • Farm shops sometimes have them too

Take big bags or a lidded box. They're light, so tie the bags firmly if they're riding in an open vehicle.

A caution when taking them

Hulls from paddies with lots of disease or weeds can carry problems into next year, so it's best to avoid them. If you can, ask where they came from.

③ Using rice hulls

1. Mix into soil for drainage and air

Mixed into soil, hard hulls leave gaps round themselves where air and water can move. They suit plots that puddle after rain, clay that sets like brick when dry, and former rice fields. The soil doesn't change at once; drainage improves gradually over several years. As the hulls slowly break down they feed soil microbes and the soil softens.

  • How much: about ½ lb per 10 sq ft (250 g per m²) — roughly 2–3 litres of hulls
  • How: spread over the surface, then mix well into the top 8 in (20 cm) with a spade or fork
  • When: at least two weeks before planting, to let the soil settle
  • Add with them: something containing nitrogen — rice bran, oilseed cake, compost (more below)

On a new plot, start below the guide amount and add more next year once you've seen how it goes.

2. Mulch against frost heave and drying

Hulls round the base of overwintering crops stop the surface getting too cold and prevent frost heave lifting the seedlings. Lifted roots dry out and the plants can die. Winter is also dry, and the mulch keeps the soil from drying out.

  • Good for: onions, garlic, strawberries, spinach, broad beans, snap peas, spring onions
  • When: before the real cold, by early winter
  • How thick: about ½ in (1 cm), enough to hide the soil
  • How: draw them round the plants as if earthing up; on mulch film, tuck them into the planting holes so they don't blow

Keep the crown of strawberries from being buried deep, but a light dusting of hulls is fine.

3. Against splashing and weeds

Hulls round the base stop rain splashing soil up. Disease organisms in the soil can reach the lower leaves on that splash, so stopping it also helps prevent disease. And with less light reaching the soil, fewer weeds come up.

4. Over a seed row

Carrots, spring onions and onions germinate unevenly if the soil dries after sowing. Sow, cover with soil, water, then sprinkle a thin layer of hulls over the row; the soil dries more slowly and the seedlings come up evenly. Don't lay it on so thick it gets in their way once they're up — just enough that soil still shows through.

5. Compost and bokashi material

Hulls can go into a compost heap with leaves, vegetable scraps and rice bran. On their own they break down very slowly, so mix with something nitrogen-rich like rice bran, add water and turn it now and then. Even so, they keep their shape longer than anything else in the heap. A bonus: they keep the heap open and airy.

6. Storing tubers

To keep sweet potatoes or taro through the winter, bury them in hulls in a cardboard box. The hulls buffer cold and drying, and being light and airy, they keep the tubers from sweating.

④ Mulching for winter, step by step

1. Weed

Pull small weeds round the plants first. Once the hulls are down, they're harder to get out.

2. Feed first

For crops that get a winter top-up (onions, garlic), give it before mulching; it's easier.

3. Draw the hulls round the plants

Scoop up hulls by hand and draw them round each plant like earthing up, about ½ in (1 cm) deep — not so deep the leaf bases are buried. On mulch film, lift the edge of each hole a little and tuck hulls deep into it too, so they don't blow.

4. Water lightly

A light sprinkle from a watering can dampens the hulls and settles them so the wind doesn't take them.

5. Add fleece before a hard frost

If a severe cold snap is forecast, drape a sheet of fleece loosely over the top as well. Layering fleece, a plastic tunnel and insect netting raises the protection further.

If frost has lifted the seedlings

Some mornings you'll find seedlings pushed up by frost, with roots showing. Left like that, the roots dry and the plants die. Once the frost has thawed, press the base gently with your fingers to put the roots back into the soil, then add more hulls round them.

⑤ Cautions when mixing hulls into soil

Make up the nitrogen

Hulls are high in carbon and very low in nitrogen. When soil microbes break them down, they use nitrogen to build their own bodies. Add lots of hulls and that breakdown can take nitrogen from the soil, leaving too little for your vegetables — "nitrogen robbery" (nitrogen drawdown). To prevent it, add something with nitrogen (rice bran, oilseed cake, poultry manure) at the same time, and mix in at least two weeks before planting to let the soil settle.

Don't overdo it

Too many hulls make the soil drain too freely and dry out fast. Added to already free-draining sandy soil, water runs straight through and vegetables go short. Once mixed in, hulls can't be taken out, so start small.

Not for free-draining soil

Mixing hulls in is a fix for poorly drained, compacting soil. On soil that already drains well, use them mainly as mulch.

Stop them blowing away

Hulls are so light that on a dry, windy day they're gone in moments. Damp them after spreading, sprinkle a little soil over them, or tuck them into planting holes. Mind they don't blow into the neighbours' garden or the road.

⑥ Matching use to your soil

Clay soil, or a former paddy

Puddles after rain, sets hard when dry. Mixing hulls in suits it best. Add them with something nitrogen-rich, a little at a time over several years. It won't change dramatically in a year, so add compost or leaf mould too and build the soil patiently.

Ordinary soil

Use hulls mainly as mulch and over seed rows. In spring or at clear-up, dig the mulch in and it gradually works into the soil.

Sandy soil

Drains freely and dries fast already. Mixing raw hulls in makes it drier still, so don't. Use them as mulch to hold moisture.

Containers

You need only a little, so charcoal is easier. A little mixed into compost improves drainage. In winter, a thin layer of hulls round the base keeps out cold and drying. Balconies are often windy, so water after spreading.

Measuring

Hulls are so light that measuring by volume is easier than by weight. At 2–3 litres per m², a quarter of a 10-litre bucket covers a little over half a tatami mat — about 10 sq ft. You'll see it's less than you'd think. Measure with a bucket rather than tipping the sack out.

⑦ Pairing hulls with rice bran

Why they go together

Hulls are high in carbon and very low in nitrogen. Rice bran is rich in nitrogen and phosphorus and is easy food for microbes. Together, the bran supplies what the hulls lack and microbes can break the hulls down more easily. Both come from rice, and rice mills often have both at the same time of year.

Mixing into soil

When you mix hulls into soil, spread a handful or so of rice bran with them and mix together. Too much bran causes smells and attracts insects, so keep it modest. Then leave at least two weeks before planting.

Making compost

  1. Hulls repel water, so soak them thoroughly first
  2. Layer or mix hulls and rice bran
  3. Moisten until a squeezed handful holds together without dripping
  4. Cover with a sheet to keep rain off
  5. When it warms up inside, mix the whole heap now and then to let air in

Hulls hold their shape for a long time, so you'll often still see them in finished compost. Once in the soil, though, they gradually return to it. It's slow — think of making it in autumn to use in spring.

⑧ About rice-hull charcoal

What it is

Rice-hull charcoal is made by slowly smouldering hulls with little air until they turn to charcoal. Burn them right through and you get ash, so the trick is stopping at the charcoal stage. It's black, free-flowing and light, and the hull shape remains.

Buying it

Garden centres sell it in small and large bags; large ones are usually cheaper for the amount, so they suit garden beds, while a small bag is plenty for a few containers. Check the label says it's made from rice hulls. Choose bags with even black grains and not too much whitish, ashy powder.

Tiny pores house microbes

Turning hulls to charcoal creates a mass of very fine pores. These pores are said to give soil microbes a home, enriching and stabilising the life in the soil — like building more houses for them.

A soil improver, not a fertiliser

The charcoal doesn't work as a fertiliser. It contains a little potassium and other minerals, but its main job is improving drainage and aeration and helping microbes. It barely breaks down, so the effect lasts.

How it differs from raw hulls

Raw hulls repel water, take ages to break down and can tie up nitrogen. Charcoal takes up water more readily, carries less nitrogen risk and is easier to handle. On the other hand, it's work to make yourself and costs money to buy.

⑨ Using rice-hull charcoal

1. Mix into soil

Mixed into garden or container soil, it improves drainage and aeration. Aim for no more than about a tenth of the soil volume, well mixed in.

2. In seedling compost

Some people mix a little into the compost they sow and raise seedlings in. It drains better and roots grow more easily.

3. As a black mulch

Being black, it soaks up sunlight and warms the soil — much like black plastic mulch. Round overwintering crops it raises soil temperature, discourages frost heave and keeps roots from being lifted.

  • Good for: onions, garlic, rakkyo (Japanese shallots), spring onion seedlings
  • How much: enough to just hide the soil; too thin and the wind takes it all at once
  • On mulch film: fill each planting hole with a small mound; in the hole, the wind just moves it around inside
  • When: before the cold, for example after a top-up feed

Being already burnt, it's said to do no harm if a little falls on the seedlings. Planting holes in mulch film are bare soil and sprout weeds easily, but charcoal in them discourages weeds too. Weeding the holes before you add it is even better. Some say cutworms and other pests dislike its smell, but it's no guarantee; treat that as a bonus.

4. In bokashi and compost

Some people add charcoal when making bokashi or plant-based compost. More homes for microbes are said to make fermentation steadier.

⑩ Making your own charcoal safely

The outline

A purpose-made charring chimney — a metal cylinder with a flue, sold in Japanese garden centres — makes it fairly straightforward.

  1. Light dry kindling or newspaper inside the chimney
  2. Once it's burning well, pile dry hulls round the chimney in a mound
  3. The heat from inside slowly blackens the hulls round it
  4. Now and then, mix the blackened hulls with the brown ones on the outside
  5. When it's all black, douse it thoroughly with water

Go too far and it turns to ash, so stop once it's all black.

The biggest danger is fire

The hulls don't burn with flames; they smoulder, giving off smoke. It looks unremarkable, but as long as there's smoke, fire is still alive inside.

  • Even after dousing, it may still be burning inside. Cut off the air, for example with a lid, leave it overnight, and make sure it's completely cold
  • Bag it while warm — in paper or plastic — and it can reignite later
  • Don't make it on windy or very dry days
  • Make it in an open space with nothing flammable nearby
  • In built-up areas, smoke and smell will bother neighbours; many places have rules about outdoor burning

It takes a supply of hulls, an open space and half a day or more. For most home gardeners, buying bags is the practical choice.

Storing charcoal

Keep the bag closed and under cover so it doesn't get wet. It's light and blows about, so open bags on a still day. It barely breaks down in soil, so once in, it stays a long time.

⑪ Crop by crop

Onions, garlic and rakkyo

Mulch round the base with hulls or charcoal to stop frost heave killing seedlings over winter. Onions dislike drying, so it also guards against winter's dry spells. Tuck it into the holes in mulch film so it doesn't blow away.

Strawberries

Mulch round the plants with hulls before the hard cold. It also stops splashing, so in spring it helps keep the fruit clean and free of disease.

Spinach, komatsuna and other winter greens

Draw hulls up along both sides of the row like earthing up. It softens the cold and stops drying. Combined with a tunnel or fleece, even better.

Broad beans and snap peas

Mulch round the base with hulls over winter against frost.

Carrots and spring onions

After sowing, sprinkle a thin layer of hulls to stop the row drying out. For spring onion seedlings over winter, charcoal round the base prevents frost heave.

Cucumbers, squash and melons

Silica, plentiful in hulls, strengthens plant tissues and is said to help against powdery mildew. But silica comes out of hulls only very slowly, so it's no quick fix. Think of it as something to add a little at a time when building the soil.

Taro and sweet potatoes

Store them over winter buried in hulls in a cardboard box.

⑫ Storing tubers, step by step

  1. Knock off soil lightly; don't wash them — washed tubers spoil faster
  2. Set damaged tubers aside to eat first; store only sound ones
  3. Spread hulls in the bottom of a cardboard box
  4. Lay the tubers so they don't touch, filling the gaps with hulls
  5. Cover the top layer until no tubers show
  6. Close the box and keep it somewhere not too cold, where the temperature stays steady

Sweet potatoes and taro both dislike cold, so don't put them anywhere that gets chilly. Open the box now and then to check for spoiled tubers, and remove any before the rot spreads.

⑬ Through the year (central Japan)

  • October–November: collect hulls after the rice harvest; mix them, with something nitrogen-rich, into poorly drained beds
  • November–December: mulch round onions, garlic, strawberries and broad beans with hulls or charcoal; use hulls for storing tubers
  • January–February: the coldest weeks. If frost has lifted seedlings, press them back and add more hulls
  • March–April: mix charcoal into seedling compost; as it warms, the winter mulch can be dug in
  • June–August: sprinkle over carrot and other seed rows against drying

⑭ Where growers disagree, and common mistakes

Does nitrogen robbery really happen? You'll often be warned that high-carbon, low-nitrogen hulls use up soil nitrogen. Others point out that hulls break down so slowly they rarely take much at once. Either way, when adding a lot, add something nitrogen-rich with them and leave time before planting.

Does charcoal correct acid soil? It's mildly alkaline and is said to soften acidity. But some trials mixing in charcoal found no clear change in soil pH. To correct acidity reliably, use lime. Think of charcoal as being for drainage and microbes.

Can raw hulls go straight into soil? Some say yes; others say compost them or turn them into charcoal first. To improve a poorly drained plot, adding raw hulls a little at a time is the easy route. To reduce nitrogen worries or make handling easier, use charcoal or compost.

Does silica make vegetables disease-resistant? Silica is said to toughen plants against disease and pests, including powdery mildew. But it leaches out of the hard husks only slowly, so you're unlikely to see an effect soon after adding them. Base disease control on good airflow and sensible feeding, and use hulls as part of long-term soil building.

Common mistakes:

  • Mixing in a huge amount at once — the soil dries out and crops struggle. You can't remove them, so start small and add next year
  • Hulls only, with no nitrogen — pale leaves and stalled growth. Add rice bran or oilseed cake
  • Mulch that all blew away — damp it, tuck it into planting holes, or sprinkle soil over it
  • Mixing them into free-draining soil — sandy soil drains even faster and plants go thirsty. Mulch there instead
  • Too thick over a seed row — it gets in the seedlings' way and they come up unevenly. Just enough that soil shows through
  • Burying the strawberry crown — a deeply buried crown rots. Just draw hulls round the plant
  • Bagging damp hulls — mould and smells. Spread wet hulls out to dry before storing
  • Bagging charcoal while hot — it can reignite. Only bag it once completely cold right through

⑮ Questions people ask

Where can I get rice hulls? In rice-growing areas, from rice mills, rice farmers and farm co-ops, easiest after the autumn harvest. Elsewhere, garden centres and hydroponics suppliers sell them.

How do I store hulls I've collected? Bagged, under cover, out of the rain. Stored wet, they go mouldy.

What do I do with the winter mulch in spring? Dig it in. A little improves drainage and aeration. If you used a lot, add a little something nitrogen-rich with it.

Can I use them in containers? Yes. A little charcoal in the compost improves drainage; in winter, hulls round the base keep out drying and cold.

Hulls or charcoal — which should I buy first? For mulching you need a lot, so cheap hulls suit best. For mixing into containers or seedling compost, or mulching just a little, charcoal is easier.

There were grains of rice in the hulls I got. A few are no problem. If rice seedlings come up where you mulched, pull them while small.

My mulch had gone darker by spring. Rain and damp mean it's slowly breaking down. Dig it in.

Is hull ash the same as hull charcoal? No. Burnt right through to ash, the fine charcoal pores are gone and the properties change. Ash is strongly alkaline, so go easy with it. Charcoal is hulls stopped at the charcoal stage, before they become ash.

How long does charcoal last? Charcoal hardly breaks down in soil, so it stays for a long time. No need to add lots every year; a little at a time is enough.

⑯ Checklist before you use them

  • Where did the hulls come from? Not a paddy with heavy disease or weeds?
  • Are they dry and free of mould?
  • Is your soil poorly drained, ordinary or sandy?
  • If mixing in, have you measured with a bucket, and got something nitrogen-rich ready?
  • Is planting at least two weeks away?
  • If mulching, have you stopped it blowing (watered it, tucked it into holes)?
  • If making charcoal, do you have open space, a still day, and time to let it cool completely?

Science bit: four whys 🔬

Why do hulls barely rot? Hulls are rich in silica and in lignin, the substance that makes plant tissues woody. Silica, a relative of glass, can't be broken down by microbes at all, and lignin takes them a very long time. So hulls keep their shape far longer than leaves or vegetable scraps.

Why are hulls so full of silica? Rice takes up a lot of silica from the soil through its roots and stores it in its leaves, stems and husks. Silica-laden tissues are hard and tough, and are thought to make the plant less prone to falling over and to damage from disease and insects. The husk around each grain is rich in silica because the plant is armouring the seed inside.

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C/N ratio) The proportion of carbon to nitrogen in organic matter is called the C/N ratio. Low C/N materials (poultry manure, rice bran) break down fast and work readily as fertiliser. High C/N materials (rice hulls, straw, fallen leaves) break down slowly and feed little, but soften the soil for a long time. Rice hulls have a very high C/N ratio. When microbes break down high-C/N material, they take the nitrogen they lack from the soil — that's how nitrogen robbery happens. Add something nitrogen-rich and the microbes take nitrogen from that instead, leaving the vegetables' share alone.

Why does mulch stop frost heave? Frost heave happens when water in the soil is drawn up towards the freezing surface and freezes into column-like ice needles. As they grow, they lift seedlings' roots along with the surface. A mulch of hulls or charcoal stops the surface getting so cold, so the needles form less readily. Charcoal, being black, also soaks up sunlight by day and warms the soil.

  • ✅ To mix in: poorly drained soil, about 2–3 litres per m² (½ lb per 10 sq ft), with something nitrogen-rich, at least two weeks before planting
  • ✅ Too much and the soil dries out — and you can't take them back out. Start small
  • ✅ Mulch against frost heave, drying and splashing; tuck into planting holes or damp them so they don't blow
  • ✅ A thin layer over a seed row stops it drying out
  • ✅ Charcoal is a soil improver that houses microbes; spread round plants, it's also a black mulch
  • ✅ Making charcoal: beware of fire. Bag it only when completely cold
  • ✅ Rice bran makes up the nitrogen hulls lack; soak hulls first when composting
